# Self-check: status lines and methods

Read this only in SELF-CHECK mode. In the report, label fields by number and name ("6- Stakes"), not by ID. Status key: STRONG does it · PARTIAL partly · MISSING not at all · N/A does not apply. The report prints them as Met, Partly met, Not met and Does not apply.

The status lines apply the handout's methods to each field; they are not printed in the handout.

## Status lines

| Field | STRONG | PARTIAL | MISSING |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 Readers | The research community is named (and wider readers, when the work has users beyond research) | Readers implied only by the venue or the jargon | Written to a general or unnamed reader |
| L2 Value words | Instability words wherever the problem is built, in the introduction and where it recurs | Instability words in only part of the problem, or community words only | No value words (flow words don't count) |
| L3 Value offered | Stated in a sentence as what readers gain | Implied, or stated only as novelty or as an activity | Not stated anywhere |
| L4 Problem | All three parts stated explicitly (current state, where it breaks, what remains open), in something these readers want to understand | A part is missing or only implied, or the problem is framed as the writer's own interest | Background or definitions only |
| L5 Stance | Makes the "you're wrong" move in the community's polite code: says what the dominant view or rival work gets wrong in measured terms (such as "incomplete"), crediting rival work before its limit | Only adds to the field ("we extend"), or breaks the code: dismisses prior work, or calls it wrong without credit | No relation to prior work or to the dominant view |
| L6 Stakes | The text says what the problem costs these readers in their own work: wrong predictions, wasted runs, a bad decision | The cost is stated only as what the model or the physics does, even when it is "their" model, or only the benefit is stated | Nothing is at risk |
| L7 Doubts answered | The main doubts these readers would raise are named and answered, with locations | Some are answered, or answered without being named | No doubt is anticipated |
| L8 Contribution | Offered as something these readers can use | Offered as an activity ("X is analyzed") or an idea to display | No contribution stated |
| L9 Structure | Problem, then prior work that sharpens it, then the contribution; explanation after value; error model; the discussion and conclusion return to the problem | One of these falls short: the literature review lists who said what, the gap model, explanation before the problem, or a conclusion that never returns to it | Opens on background or generalizations (the martini glass) and never builds a problem |
| L10 Pitfalls | All four avoided | One present | Two or more present |
| L11 Value count | 8 or more distinct value words in the first two paragraphs | 4–7 | 3 or fewer |
| L12 Opening | The first sentence starts from the readers' problem, the question the paper answers appears in the first paragraph, and no later section states the problem more sharply | The question first appears after the first paragraph, or a later section states the problem more sharply | The paper never states the problem it solves |

L5: saying what prior work addressed or achieved counts as credit. A direct challenge is fine when its words are measured (M9). Work the paper builds on, such as evidence it agrees with, needs no limit; only the views and approaches it challenges do.

L11 counts distinct community and instability words (M6); flow words don't count. McEnerney's rough target is about ten.

STRONG and N/A fields need no fix move; name the method that the evidence shows, if useful.

**Priority fix.** The one field that most limits value. When several fall short, the problem (L4) comes first, then the stakes (L6), then the opening (L12): readers who can't see a problem and what it costs them stop reading before polish, or their doubts, matter.

## Methods behind fix moves

Use one to choose each fix. The report states the fix in plain words and never prints the code.

- **M1 Value-oriented writing:** ask why these readers should care; signal value with value words; keep a word bank from your field's journals.
- **M2 Know your readers:** their beliefs, assumptions and doubts; how your target journals state costs and benefits.
- **M3 Constructing problems:** move from background or definitions to instability; build problems that matter to the readers and challenge the field's assumptions or practices.
- **M4 Argument vs explanation:** argue to change what the readers think; predict and answer their doubts.
- **M5 Literature reviews build problems:** show the tensions between works; prefer the error model ("this has been done, but there's a problem") to the gap model ("this hasn't been done").
- **M6 Two kinds of value words:** community words (widely, accepted, reported) show readers already care; instability words (nonetheless, however, although, inconsistent, anomaly) create tension; flow words (because, and, if, unless) create none; "rarely" often signals a gap claim.
- **M7 Value before explanation:** explain only after value is established; don't open with a definition; don't answer "this doesn't seem important" by explaining more.
- **M8 Openings that show a problem:** no martini-glass opening; readers scan the opening for instability; state the problem the thesis solves, in something the readers want to understand.
- **M9 "You're wrong" in the community's code:** show that the community has something wrong, in the polite way its journals say it; skipping the move gets a paper rejected.
- **M10 Raising the costs:** credit the community's work, then show the tension matters because the community has built on it; gap claims add little.
